# Instagram/TikTok Comment Data for VoC Research - Official Route and Legal/Risk Picture

Research date - 11 September 2026. This is a research summary, not legal advice. Every claim below is sourced with a URL and marked either as a direct quote/paraphrase of a source, or as UNVERIFIED where no reliable source was found.

---

## Part 1 - The Official Instagram Route

### 1. Instagram Graph API - can it return the full comment thread (including replies) on the connected business account's own media?

Yes, for the connected account's own media, with a caveat on how replies are fetched.

- Endpoint - `GET /{ig-media-id}/comments`. Per Meta's Graph API reference, this "returns only top-level comments. Replies to comments are not included unless you use field expansion to request the `replies` field." Replies themselves live on a separate edge, `GET /{ig-comment-id}/replies`.
  Source - https://developers.facebook.com/docs/instagram-platform/instagram-graph-api/reference/ig-media/comments (read via WebFetch, 11 Sep 2026)
  Source - https://developers.facebook.com/docs/marketing-api/reference/instagram-comment/replies (11 Sep 2026)

- Fields - the official example response for the comments edge shows `id`, `text`, `timestamp`. Third-party developer guides (not Meta-official, so treat as corroborating rather than authoritative) show a fuller field set is selectable via the `fields` query parameter, e.g. `fields=like_count,replies,username,text`. UNVERIFIED against the primary Meta reference page directly (the fetched page content did not itself list `like_count` or `username`, though these are long-documented fields of the Instagram comment node in community tutorials).
  Source - https://www.justinstolpe.com/blog_code/instagram_graph_api/comments_and_replies.php (11 Sep 2026, non-official)
  Source (official, partial) - https://developers.facebook.com/docs/instagram-platform/instagram-graph-api/reference/ig-media/comments (11 Sep 2026)

- Permissions - `instagram_basic` and `instagram_manage_comments` (older Facebook-Login-for-Business scope names; Meta's 2025 rename replaced these with `instagram_business_basic` and `instagram_business_manage_comments` for the newer Instagram-Login/Business-Login surface). The old scopes were deprecated 27 January 2025; apps that had not migrated stopped being able to call Instagram endpoints.
  Source - https://developers.facebook.com/docs/instagram-platform/instagram-graph-api/reference/ig-media/comments (11 Sep 2026)
  Source - Elfsight developer guide summarizing the 2025 scope rename, https://elfsight.com/blog/instagram-graph-api-changes/ (11 Sep 2026, non-official, corroborating)

- Rate limits - the comments edge itself returns "a maximum of 50 comments per query" and "comments cannot be filtered by timestamp" (pagination needed for full threads). General platform rate limiting for the Instagram Graph API is commonly cited at 200 calls per hour per user access token in third-party guides; this session could not verify that exact figure against a primary Meta rate-limit page, so treat the 200/hour figure as UNVERIFIED (widely repeated across secondary sources, not confirmed against Meta's own rate-limit documentation in this research pass).
  Source - https://developers.facebook.com/docs/instagram-platform/instagram-graph-api/reference/ig-media/comments (11 Sep 2026)
  Source (200/hour claim, unverified) - https://elfsight.com/blog/instagram-graph-api-complete-developer-guide-for-2026/ (11 Sep 2026)

- App Review for a company reading its own comments - Meta operates two access levels. "Standard Access is the default access level for all apps and limits the data your app can get, intended for apps used only by people with roles on them, during development, or testing. If your app only serves your own Instagram professional account or an account you manage, Standard Access is all your app needs" - meaning no App Review is required if the business is only ever reading its own account's comments and the relevant people are added as the app's testers/roles. Advanced Access (needed to read OTHER accounts' data at scale) requires full Meta App Review plus Business Verification.
  Source - developer summary drawing on Meta's Access Levels documentation, https://developers.facebook.com/docs/instagram-platform/overview/ and related pages, corroborated by https://singhamandeep.com/what-is-meta-advanced-access/ (11 Sep 2026). Direct fetch of Meta's own App Review / Access Levels pages returned HTTP 404 in this session (URLs have moved); this conclusion rests on secondary developer sources describing the current Meta documentation, so treat the exact "no review needed for own account" framing as reasonably well corroborated but not confirmed against a primary Meta URL in this session.

### 2. Business Discovery and Hashtag Search - text or counts only, for OTHER accounts' media?

**Business Discovery - counts only, no comment text.** The Business Discovery endpoint (used to inspect another public Business/Creator account) exposes only `comments_count`, `like_count`, and `view_count` on that account's media - not the underlying comment text. Meta's own documentation adds an important access barrier on top of that: "this does not grant you permission to access media objects directly - performing a GET on any returned IG Media will fail due to insufficient permissions." In other words, even the numeric fields exist only as nested read-only expansions off Business Discovery; you cannot pivot from a discovered media ID to that media's `/comments` edge.
Source - https://developers.facebook.com/docs/instagram-platform/instagram-graph-api/business-discovery (fetched via WebFetch, 11 Sep 2026 - direct quotes above are verbatim from the fetched Meta page)

**Hashtag Search - counts only, no comment text, and no username on returned media.** The flow is `GET /ig_hashtag_search?user_id=...&hashtag=...` to resolve a hashtag ID, then `GET /{hashtag-id}/recent_media` or `/top_media`. Per Meta's documentation (as summarized by developer sources quoting it): this edge "only returns public photos and videos" published "within 24 hours of query execution" for recent_media, capped at 50 results per page, with example fields limited to `id, media_type, comments_count, like_count`. Critically: "You cannot request the username field on returned media objects, and responses will not include any personally identifiable information." A business can query a maximum of 30 unique hashtags in a rolling 7-day window, and users cannot comment on hashtagged media discovered this way. Access requires Instagram Public Content Access, which is an Advanced Access permission requiring App Review.
Source - https://developers.facebook.com/docs/instagram-api/reference/hashtag/recent-media and https://developers.facebook.com/docs/instagram-platform/instagram-graph-api/reference/ig-hashtag/recent-media (referenced and quoted via WebSearch summary of Meta's documentation, 11 Sep 2026)

**Conclusion for VoC research on competitor/other brands' posts:** neither Business Discovery nor Hashtag Search will get you actual comment text on media you do not own. Both are counts-only (or, for Hashtag Search, media metadata only) by design.

### 3. Mentions and tagged media - can the API return comments on OTHER accounts' posts where the connected account is mentioned?

Narrow yes, but only for the mention/tag itself, not for the wider comment thread. The Mentions guide "explains how to identify IG Comments and IG Media in which an IG User has been tagged or @mentioned" on OTHER users' media. Concretely:
- `GET /{ig-user-id}?fields=mentioned_comment` - retrieves the specific comment where the connected account was @mentioned (that one comment, not the whole thread).
- `GET /{ig-user-id}/tags` - retrieves media objects where the connected account has been tagged.
- Real-time delivery is also available via Webhooks, which "explains how to get real-time notifications whenever Instagram users comment on any of an IG User's IG Media objects, @mention an IG User on other Instagram users' IG Media objects."

This is not a general-purpose route to read all comments on someone else's post - it surfaces only the individual comment/media where the connected account is directly tagged or mentioned, triggered by that mention event, and (per the same Meta developer documentation on Public Content Access) still sits behind the same App Review gate as other cross-account data.
Source - Meta developer documentation on Mentions and Webhooks, summarized/quoted via WebSearch, primary pages at https://developers.facebook.com/docs/instagram-platform (specific mentions guide sub-pages returned 404 on direct WebFetch in this session; conclusions drawn from search-engine-indexed summaries of that documentation, 11 Sep 2026) - treat sub-detail as reasonably reliable but not independently re-verified by this session against the live page.

### 4. Practical setup - Live mode + System User token, or is Advanced Access token enough? Shortest path for a company reading its own comments from a script?

- For an app that only ever touches accounts the business itself owns/manages (which is the scenario here - reading your own company's post comments), Meta's documented Standard Access level applies, and Standard Access "covers only role-assigned accounts" and does not require App Review. This is the shortest supported path: register a Meta developer app, add the Instagram Business/Creator account (linked to a Facebook Page) as a Page role / app tester, generate a User Access Token via the Graph API Explorer with `instagram_basic` + `instagram_manage_comments` (or their 2025-renamed equivalents) and `pages_show_list`, and call `/{ig-media-id}/comments`. The app can remain in Development mode for this because only manually-added testers/roles are needed.
  Source - synthesis of Meta Access Levels documentation as summarized by developer guides, e.g. https://singhamandeep.com/what-is-meta-advanced-access/ and https://elfsight.com/blog/instagram-graph-api-complete-developer-guide-for-2026/ (11 Sep 2026)

- A System User token from Business Manager is the recommended approach specifically for unattended/server-side scripts (as opposed to a per-user OAuth login flow), because User Access Tokens are tied to a human login session and a System User token is designed for server-to-server automation. It is not a strictly required step - a long-lived User Access Token, refreshed periodically, can also work for a script - but System User tokens avoid the token-refresh/expiry fragility of user tokens and are Meta's documented pattern for this use case.
  Source - developer summaries of Meta's System Users documentation (a direct fetch of https://developers.facebook.com/docs/development/build-and-test/system-users returned HTTP 404 in this session - the URL appears to have moved/changed - so this is drawn from secondary sources describing that documentation, e.g. https://support.infosum.com/hc/en-us/articles/21987155916306, 11 Sep 2026). Treat as UNVERIFIED against a primary Meta URL in this session.

- Live mode / App Review is NOT required purely to read your own account's comments via a script, per the above Standard Access framing. Live mode and Advanced Access (full App Review + Business Verification) are required only once the app needs to read data on accounts it does not own/manage (e.g. to read comments on OTHER businesses' posts) - which loops back to the Business Discovery/Hashtag Search limitations in Question 2: even with full Advanced Access, Meta's API still will not hand over comment TEXT on other accounts' media, only counts. So there is no tier of official access, however much review is completed, that returns comment text on competitors' or other companies' posts.

## Part 2 - Legal and Account Risk of Third-Party Scraping (Apify) of Public Comments

### 5. Meta's Platform Terms and Instagram Terms of Use wording on automated collection/scraping

**Meta's Automated Data Collection Terms** (a standalone Meta legal document, distinct from the general Platform Terms, aimed specifically at scraping):
- Definition - "'Automated Data Collection' refers to the collection of data from Meta Company Products via the use of automated or programmatic tools capable of navigating or indexing the surface-layer of the World Wide Web. These tools may include but are not limited to web scrapers, bots, robots, spiders, crawlers, user-agents, and other automated or programmatic mechanisms designed to access, collect, or retrieve data or content directly from web pages or applications."
- Permitted uses are narrowly limited to: (i) "providing results for your Search Engine," (ii) "displaying previews of Meta URLs to your users," or (iii) other uses with Meta's "express written permission."
- Prohibitions include "transferring, selling, licensing or sublicensing Collected Data and data derived from Collected Data to any third party," restrictions on scraping non-public personal data (only "Publicly Available Personal Data" is contemplated at all), and a requirement to respect "Meta's robots.txt protocols, page header tags, and similar opt-out protocols." Meta also reserves the right to "implement measures to restrict your Automated Data Collection ... at any time for any reason."
Source - https://www.facebook.com/legal/automated_data_collection_terms (fetched directly via WebFetch, quotes verbatim, 11 Sep 2026)

- A related, frequently-quoted clause of Meta's general Platform/user terms (as characterized in secondary legal commentary rather than fetched verbatim from a live Terms page in this session) prohibits users from accessing "or collect[ing] data from our Products using automated means (without our prior permission) or attempt[ing] to access data you do not have permission to access," and clarifies that merely accepting the terms does not itself constitute the required written permission.
Source - summarized/quoted via WebSearch of secondary legal analysis, e.g. https://thunderbit.com/blog/is-scraping-facebook-legal and https://www.zyte.com/blog/california-court-meta-ruling/ (11 Sep 2026) - this specific quote is UNVERIFIED against a live, directly-fetched Meta Terms of Service page in this session (two direct WebFetch attempts at Instagram's Terms of Use URL, https://www.instagram.com/legal/terms/ and its help.instagram.com redirect target, returned only page-shell content with no policy text, likely because the page is a JavaScript-rendered single-page app that WebFetch's HTML-to-markdown conversion could not render).

- This is consistent with the central holding of Meta v. Bright Data (see Q6): the court found the Facebook/Instagram Terms, as written, only bind logged-in "Users" and do not reach logged-out, no-account scraping of public pages - i.e., the terms' scraping prohibition is real and clearly worded, but a federal court has found it contractually inapplicable to a specific, common mode of scraping (logged-out).

### 6. Meta v. Bright Data (N.D. Cal.) and current status as of 2026; hiQ v. LinkedIn

**Meta v. Bright Data.** Meta sued Bright Data in 2023 alleging breach of contract and tortious interference, over Bright Data scraping and reselling data from Facebook and Instagram (notwithstanding that Meta had itself been a Bright Data customer for scraping other sites). In January 2024, Senior US District Judge Edward Chen (N.D. Cal.) granted summary judgment to Bright Data on the breach-of-contract claim. Bright Data's argument, which the court accepted, was "that Meta's terms apply only to data behind the password wall and limit user activity only when logged in" - so a party that scrapes public Facebook/Instagram pages while logged out is not a "User" bound by those terms at all, and therefore cannot have breached them, "and therefore cannot bar Bright Data's logged-off scraping activities."

In February/March 2024, Meta filed to dismiss its one remaining claim (tortious interference, which turned largely on Bright Data's customers' use of the data) and separately agreed to waive its right to appeal the summary judgment ruling. As of the most recent information available in this research (through August 2026), the case does not appear in Meta's subsequent SEC 10-Q filings (which do list its other current major litigation), and no source located in this research indicates any appeal, refiling, or reopening. The matter appears fully closed, with the summary-judgment ruling standing unreviewed as persuasive precedent that Meta's Terms do not reach logged-out scraping of public data.
Source - https://www.courthousenews.com/federal-judge-rules-against-meta-in-data-scraping-case/ ; https://techcrunch.com/2024/02/26/meta-drops-lawsuit-against-web-scraping-firm-bright-data-that-sold-millions-of-instagram-records ; https://brightdata.com/blog/general/meta-dismisses-claim-against-bright-data ; https://www.fbm.com/publications/major-decision-affects-law-of-scraping-and-online-data-collection-meta-platforms-v-bright-data/ (all via WebSearch, 11 Sep 2026)

**hiQ Labs, Inc. v. LinkedIn Corp. (9th Cir.), in one paragraph.** LinkedIn sent hiQ a cease-and-desist over hiQ's scraping of public LinkedIn profile data; hiQ sued and won a preliminary injunction. On 18 April 2022, the Ninth Circuit (on remand, following the Supreme Court's Van Buren decision) reaffirmed that scraping data from a website's publicly accessible pages - i.e., pages that do not require login - does not violate the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act's "without authorization" prong, reasoning that a public website has no access "gates" to lift or lower in the first place. That CFAA win did not end the case favorably for hiQ, however: on remand on the separate contract theory, the district court found hiQ had breached LinkedIn's User Agreement (and had additionally used fake accounts/contractors to access some non-public data), and the parties ultimately settled with hiQ paying damages and destroying its scraped data. The case remains the most-cited US precedent for "scraping public, logged-out data does not violate the CFAA," while also standing as a caution that a separate breach-of-contract claim can still succeed even where the CFAA claim fails.
Source - https://calawyers.org/privacy-law/ninth-circuit-holds-data-scraping-is-legal-in-hiq-v-linkedin/ ; https://www.fenwick.com/insights/publications/hiq-labs-scrapes-by-again-the-ninth-circuit-reaffirms-that-data-scraping-does-not-violate-the-cfaa-1 ; https://www.jdsupra.com/legalnews/data-scraping-in-hiq-v-linkedin-the-5900375 (all via WebSearch, 11 Sep 2026)

### 7. Enforcement against Apify specifically, or against customers of scraping services; does Meta suspend accounts of businesses that BUY scraped data?

**Against Apify specifically - UNVERIFIED / not found.** This research found no reported lawsuit, cease-and-desist, or other enforcement action by Meta or TikTok specifically targeting Apify. Apify is not named as a defendant in any of the located Meta or TikTok scraping litigation; Apify instead appears in search results as a scraping-tool vendor (including for Meta and TikTok data) that has publicly commented favorably on the Bright Data ruling. Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence here - this is a fast-moving area and a fresh, unindexed action cannot be ruled out - but nothing found in this session supports a claim of Meta or TikTok enforcement against Apify by name.
Source - multiple WebSearch queries targeting "Meta v Apify," "TikTok v Apify," "cease and desist Apify" returned no matching results, 11 Sep 2026.

**Against other scraping/scraping-for-hire services - well documented.** Meta has repeatedly and successfully enforced against scraping SERVICES (as distinct from end-customers of scraped data):
- Meta v. Voyager Labs (filed Jan 2023) - Meta alleged Voyager created 38,000+ fake Facebook accounts to scrape data (including comments) from over 600,000 users for law-enforcement/surveillance clients. Meta disabled Voyager's accounts and won a court ruling letting the case proceed; the parties settled with Voyager Labs agreeing to a permanent injunction against scraping Facebook/Instagram, a monetary payment to Meta, and permanent deletion of collected data.
  Source - https://www.cnbc.com/2023/01/12/meta-sues-voyager-labs-over-scraping-user-data ; https://www.courthousenews.com/meta-can-proceed-with-data-scraping-suit-against-uk-surveillance-startup/ ; https://about.fb.com/news/2023/01/leading-the-fight-against-scraping-for-hire/ (11 Sep 2026)
- Meta v. BrandTotal and Unimania (settled 2022) - both permanently banned from scraping/using Facebook and Instagram, and required to pay Meta a "significant financial sum."
  Source - https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/meta-launches-new-legal-action-against-data-scraping-companies/640336/ (11 Sep 2026)
- Meta's Bug Bounty "Data Abuse" program states that where a third-party app "bought, sold, disclosed, transferred, or used user data in any manner prohibited by policies or terms," Meta's enforcement options include "shutting down the offending platform app, taking legal action, or an onsite forensic audit of the company selling or buying the data" - language that at least contemplates action against a data BUYER, though this appears targeted at apps that had platform access to begin with (i.e., that themselves collected user data via Meta's platform under a data-use agreement), not at an unrelated third party that separately purchases scraped comment data from a vendor like Apify.
  Source - https://bugbounty.meta.com/data-abuse/ (11 Sep 2026, referenced via WebSearch)

**Does Meta suspend the accounts of ordinary businesses that BUY scraped public data (as distinct from the scrapers themselves)? UNVERIFIED.** No source located in this research documents a case of Meta suspending or disabling a business's own Instagram/Facebook account specifically because that business purchased or used scraped public-comment data from a third-party vendor (as opposed to the business itself running the automated collection, or being a scraping vendor). This is a materially different risk profile from "Meta sues the scraper" and this research found nothing directly on point. Flag this to the client as an open question rather than a settled "no risk" or "high risk" answer.

### 8. Apify's published legal position, and its terms on customer responsibility

**Apify's public "is scraping legal" position:** "Web scraping is legal if you scrape data that is publicly available on the internet," with caveats that "you should be careful when scraping personal data or intellectual [property]," and encouragement to consider ethics beyond bare legality (would the data subject mind; does it serve a legitimate/public interest). Apify includes a lawyer-disclaimer: "We are lawyers, but we are not your lawyers. Even though we want to help as much as we can, we do not know the details of your project" - i.e., its legal-position content is general education, not advice for any specific customer's project.
Source - https://blog.apify.com/is-web-scraping-legal/ (fetched via WebFetch, quotes verbatim, 11 Sep 2026)

**Apify's General Terms and Conditions - what the customer is responsible for:**
- "You are solely responsible for the legality, accuracy, quality, appropriateness, and use of all Customer Data." (Customer Data clause)
- "You must use the Services to process only the Customer Data that you are authorized to access and that is in compliance with all applicable laws and regulations." (Authorized Use clause)
- Indemnification - "You agree to indemnify, defend and hold us [Apify], our agents, affiliates, subsidiaries, directors, officers, employees, and applicable third parties harmless from and against any third-party claim, liability, loss, and expense ... arising out of (i) your use of the Website or Services in breach of the Agreement; and (ii) your publication or use of any Actors [Apify's term for scraper tools/scripts]."
- Apify's own liability disclaimer - "We shall not be liable for the contents of the information that you upload on our Website, or Services, including Customer Data or Actors. We are not obliged to verify the manner in which you or other Users or customers use the Website or Services, and we shall not be liable for the manner of such usage."

Net effect: Apify's terms place legal responsibility for choosing lawful scraping targets, complying with the target site's own terms/robots.txt, and complying with data protection law squarely on the customer, and require the customer to indemnify Apify for any resulting third-party claim - including, per the terms' own wording, a customer's own choice to "extract Customer Data from unauthorized sources."
Source - https://docs.apify.com/legal/general-terms-and-conditions (fetched via WebFetch, quotes verbatim, 11 Sep 2026)

### 9. Australian Privacy Act 1988 / Australian Privacy Principles - is a public comment plus handle "personal information"? What obligations attach?

**Yes - publicly posted content is still "personal information" under Australian law**, and the OAIC has directly and repeatedly rejected the "it's already public so it doesn't count" argument:
- OAIC social media guidance - "Even if personal information is publicly available (for example, if it's published on social media, or in a phone directory on a website), it is still covered by the Privacy Act." The same guidance separately notes that comments "you've made on other people's pages" can persist and remain relevant even after a user deactivates their own account - underscoring that a commenter's own privacy interest in their public comment doesn't evaporate.
  Source - https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/your-privacy-rights/social-media-and-online-privacy/social-media (via WebSearch summary of OAIC content, 11 Sep 2026)
- OAIC's Australian Privacy Principles Guidelines, Chapter 3 (APP 3 - Collection): "Where personal information is publicly available on the internet, that does not allow it to be collected and used in whatever way the APP entity chooses without regard to the knowledge and reasonable expectations of the person whose information it concerns." A comment plus a handle is near-certainly "personal information" as defined (information about an identified or reasonably identifiable individual), and could shade into "sensitive information" (special category, e.g., if the comment reveals health, political, or similar attributes) which carries a stricter consent requirement.
  Source - https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/australian-privacy-principles/australian-privacy-principles-guidelines/chapter-3-app-3-collection-of-solicited-personal-information (fetched via WebFetch, quote verbatim, 11 Sep 2026)

**APP 3 (Collection) obligations that would attach to collecting Instagram/TikTok comments for VoC research:** collection must be "only by lawful and fair means" and only where "reasonably necessary" for the entity's functions/activities (data minimisation) - and, where practicable, "directly from the individual" rather than via a third party such as a scraping vendor, unless direct collection is unreasonable or impracticable (which, for comments on someone else's public post, plausibly is impracticable - but this is a judgment call, not a blanket exemption).
Source - https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/australian-privacy-principles/australian-privacy-principles-guidelines/chapter-3-app-3-collection-of-solicited-personal-information (fetched via WebFetch, 11 Sep 2026)

**APP 5 (Notification) obligations:** an entity collecting personal information (including indirectly, i.e., via a scraping vendor rather than directly from the commenter) must, "at or before the time" of collection or as soon as practicable after, take reasonable steps to notify the individual of, or otherwise ensure they are aware of, matters including the fact and circumstances of collection - and where the information came from another entity (here, effectively, "the internet" via a scraper), the individual "should be made aware of the name of the entity" providing it "or, if impractical, the kinds of entities from which it collects that information." For comments scraped without any direct relationship to the commenter, satisfying APP 5 in a literal, individual-by-individual sense is difficult in practice - this is a genuine compliance friction point for comment-scraping-for-research use cases, not a solved problem, and this research offers no view on how a specific research use would be assessed; that is a matter for the business's own privacy compliance advice.
Source - https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/australian-privacy-principles/australian-privacy-principles-guidelines/chapter-5-app-5-notification-of-the-collection-of-personal-information (fetched via WebFetch, 11 Sep 2026)

**Small business exemption (AUD 3 million turnover threshold) - status as of 11 September 2026.** The Privacy Act 1988 currently exempts most businesses with annual turnover of AUD 3 million or less from most of the Act (with standing exceptions, e.g. health service providers, entities that trade in personal information, and a few others regardless of turnover). Reform is actively underway but this specific exemption has NOT yet been repealed by passed legislation as of the most recent evidence found:
- The first tranche, the Privacy and Other Legislation Amendment Act 2024, received Royal Assent 10 December 2024 and is in force, but did not itself remove the small business exemption.
- Attorney-General Michelle Rowland told Senate Estimates in February 2026 that the government is "progressing" a second tranche of reforms (which is expected to address the small business exemption) but gave no timeline for introducing a Bill.
- By late August 2026, the government had released only an Exposure Draft (the "Privacy Amendment (Personal Data Protection) Bill 2026") for consultation - not enacted legislation.
- Some lower-quality secondary sources found in this research incorrectly claim the exemption has "already been removed" or "passed" - these appear to be either mistaken or promotional content and conflict with the more authoritative, directly-quoted Attorney-General statements; this report treats the exemption as NOT YET REPEALED as of 11 September 2026, but flags this as a fast-moving area the client should re-check directly at oaic.gov.au or the Attorney-General's Department before relying on it.
  Source - https://www.nortonrosefulbright.com/en/knowledge/publications/be98b0ff/australian-privacy-alert-parliament-passes-major-and-meaningful-privacy-law-reform ; https://weblegal.ai/en/blog/australia-privacy-act-2026-small-business-exemption/ ; https://www.epicit.com.au/privacy-act-2026-small-business/ (conflicting claims noted; all via WebSearch, 11 Sep 2026) - **status of the exemption itself should be treated as UNVERIFIED-PENDING against a primary OAIC/legislation.gov.au source; this session did not directly fetch the OAIC's own small-business guidance page or the Bill text.**

**Practical read for a small AU marketing company:** if the company (or its client, if the data collection is done on the client's behalf) has turnover under AUD 3 million and is not one of the excepted categories, it may currently fall outside the Privacy Act's coverage for this activity - but that status is under active legislative threat and could change within the lifetime of any project built on it, and the OAIC's own view (that the exemption is "no longer appropriate") signals the direction of travel. This is not legal advice; a company relying on the small business exemption should confirm its own turnover-based status and monitor the Tranche 2 reform directly rather than relying on this summary.

## Overall summary for the client

- **Official Instagram route** gets you full comment text, replies, and engagement metadata - but only for posts YOUR OWN connected business account owns, via `/{ig-media-id}/comments` + `/{ig-comment-id}/replies`, achievable with Standard Access (no App Review) if it is only ever your own accounts. There is no officially sanctioned, review-gated, or paid tier of the Instagram API that will hand over comment TEXT on other companies' posts - Business Discovery and Hashtag Search are hard-capped at counts/metadata only, by design, regardless of access level. If the VoC research goal specifically requires reading comments on OTHER brands' or competitors' posts, the official API route cannot deliver that at all - only your own brand's posts.
- **Scraping via Apify for other companies' public comments** sits in a genuinely contested legal space: two major US rulings (hiQ v. LinkedIn on CFAA; Meta v. Bright Data on contract law) both favor the position that scraping logged-out, public data is not unlawful under those specific causes of action, and Meta has since declined to appeal the Bright Data loss. But both platforms' written Terms of Service still explicitly prohibit automated scraping without permission, both platforms have a track record of aggressively enforcing against scraping SERVICES (though not, on the evidence found here, against Apify by name, or against end-customers merely for buying scraped data), and Apify's own terms place all legal responsibility for target selection and compliance onto the customer, with an indemnification obligation running from customer to Apify. Separately and independently of any US case law, Australian Privacy Act obligations (APP 3 collection, APP 5 notification) apply in full to any comment-plus-handle data the company collects for its own market research, unless the company qualifies for the (currently still in-force, but actively threatened) small business exemption.
